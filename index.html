            What this assumes, and what it is not
            
      
              
    • 
                This is a first pass, not a design. Ground support carrying an
                LED wall over people's heads is life-safety kit. A real one is signed off by a
                structural engineer against the manufacturer's load data and a wind standard —
                ANSI E1.21 for outdoor temporary structures, EN 13782, with the wind itself
                from ASCE 7 or EN 1991-1-4. Use this to sketch a scheme, sanity-check
                someone else's numbers, or see which lever matters most.
              
      • 
              
    • 
                It checks overturning only. It says nothing about whether the truss
                can carry the load, whether the baseplate or the wall's framing can take the
                bending, or what the connections see. The wall hanging off the front face
                puts a permanent bending moment into each upright that wants checking
                separately.
              
      • 
              
    • 
                Wind pressure is taken as ½ρv² times a force coefficient, with the resultant
                at the middle of the wall. Design codes add gust, exposure, height and
                importance factors on top, and a real assessment uses them.
              
      • 
              
    • 
                The whole run is treated as one rigid body tipping about the line of the
                baseplate edges, with the load shared evenly between uprights. That is the
                global check. Individual bases still want checking for uplift, and the end
                bays carry less than the middle ones.
              
      • 
              
    • 
                If the wall's bottom row bears on the ground, that only helps where its own
                footing reaches further forward than the baseplate does — and then only if
                the cabinets can actually take being stood on, which is a load path most are
                not designed for. Where the bearing point falls inside the baseplate, it
                makes no difference at all: the contact lifts off the instant the thing
                starts to go over. It does relieve weight and bending in the truss either
                way, which is a separate question from overturning.
              
      • 
              
    • 
                Ground is flat, level and hard, and the baseplates cannot slide or sink. No
                allowance for a raked stage, a soft field, or a wall built out of plumb.
              
      • 
              
    • 
                Nothing here accounts for guy lines, rear rakers, outriggers, or tying into a
                structure — all of which change the answer completely, usually for the better.
              
      • 
            
    
          
    

          
    
            What this assumes
            
      
              
    • 
                Everything is rigid and the pole is fixed solidly to the plate — no bending,
                no flexing, no wobbling in its socket.
              
      • 
              
    • 
                The ground is flat, level and hard. A plate sinking into soft ground or
                resting on a slope behaves differently.
              
      • 
              
    • 
                The push is steady and slow. A sharp knock can tip something that a steady
                push of the same size would not, which is what the energy figure is for.
              
      • 
              
    • 
                The baseplate is a uniform slab and the pole a uniform rod, both with their
                weight evenly spread, and the pole is centred on the plate.
              
      • 
              
    • 
                The force is the level at which it starts to lift. That is also the
                hardest part — once it is past the balance point it keeps going on its own,
                and needs less and less force on the way over.
              
      • 
              
    • 
                It answers "will it tip", not "will it break". A tall pole may bend or shear
                at its base well before the plate lifts.
              
      • 
              
    • 
                In the simulation, the push keeps pointing the same way as the object turns,
                and the pivot edge is taken as staying put once it has lifted. A push aimed
                at a corner is simulated as the equivalent flat-on problem, which gets the
                tipping point and the lean exactly right but only approximates how quickly
                it falls.
